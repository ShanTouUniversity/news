The Shantou University Office of Resource Management has published health information on chickenpox, covering the disease’s pathogen and transmission, incubation period, common symptoms, possible complications, care, and prevention. The guidance draws attention to the highly contagious respiratory disease.

### Transmission and symptoms

The notice explains that chickenpox is caused by the varicella-zoster virus. Patients are the only source of infection and are generally contagious from one to two days before symptoms begin until the skin lesions have dried and crusted. The virus is transmitted mainly through respiratory droplets and contact. People of any age can become infected, with children more commonly affected; the incubation period is generally 10–21 days.

Early symptoms may include a mild fever, discomfort, and reduced appetite. Small red spots may then appear on the chest and abdomen and spread across the body, developing into fluid-filled blisters that break and crust. The notice says that rashes may appear in successive waves over several days and usually shed over 10–14 days.

### Complications and care

The notice describes chickenpox as generally mild but advises particular caution for adults, people with chronic illnesses, people with leukemia or cancer, and those taking steroid medication, because the disease can lead to complications such as pneumonia and encephalitis.

The guidance recommends isolating patients immediately and maintaining isolation at least until all lesions have crusted. It also advises rest during fever, avoiding broken blisters caused by scratching, keeping the skin clean, bathing with lukewarm water, drinking adequate fluids, and eating easily digestible food. It specifically cautions against using aspirin to reduce fever because of the associated complication risk.

### Prevention

The notice recommends isolating patients until all lesions have crusted, limiting susceptible people’s contact with patients, and discusses preventive measures including live attenuated varicella vaccination. Current vaccination, isolation, and treatment decisions should follow the advice of health authorities and medical professionals.
